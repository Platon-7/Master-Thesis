\chapter{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a principled framework for teaching robots to act through trial and error. However, it requires defining a reward function that not only specifies what the robot should achieve, but also gives guidance, along the way, on how to achieve it \citep{ng_policy_1999}. For robotic manipulation, designing this signal by hand is notoriously laborious and brittle. Dense, shaped rewards demand task-specific engineering and privileged state information such as object poses, contact flags, or goal distances, which is rarely available outside simulation. Sparse success rewards, by contrast, are easier to specify but hard to optimize. Furthermore, a reward crafted for one task seldom transfers to the next, so every new behavior reopens the same engineering problem. In practice, the real bottleneck is usually the reward and not the policy optimizer. A good reward lets the same RL loop learn quickly, while a bad one sinks it regardless of the algorithm.

Vision-language models (VLMs) trained on internet-scale data have recently shown broad visual and semantic understanding that transfers across domains with little or no task-specific tuning \citep{radford_learning_2021, flamingo_2022, bai_qwen-vl_2023, liu_visual_2023}. A single such model can caption an image, answer questions about a scene, and follow instructions grounded in what it sees, often at a level that was out of reach only a few years ago \citep{openai_gpt-4_2023}. Beyond static images, these foundation models are increasingly being grounded in physical environments to drive embodied reasoning and agent control \citep{driess_palm-e_2023}. This success raises a natural question. Can state-of-the-art VLMs provide meaningful, generalizable rewards and resolve the reward-definition bottleneck in robot RL, building on recent breakthroughs in automated reward engineering \citep{ma_eureka_2024}?

Several recent works pursue this idea, fine-tuning pretrained VLMs to predict rewards for robot behavior \citep{robodopamine, roboreward, TRI-reward-model}. The most competitive of these is Robometer \citep{robometer}, a large multi-task vision-language reward model trained on a corpus of over a million robot trajectories and equipped with dedicated output heads for success, task-progress prediction, and trajectory comparison. Robometer reports strong offline reward-quality metrics and also closes the loop by using its reward to train RL policies in both simulation and the real world. Its simulated results, however, come with an important asterisk. They do not use the reward model off the shelf. Before the RL evaluation, they adapt it to the target domain, fine-tuning it with LoRA \citep{hu_lora_2022} on LIBERO demonstrations. Their evaluation therefore never tests an unadapted vision-language reward model driving simulated RL. This gap matters, because in-domain adaptation can hide how poorly a reward transfers to the states a policy actually visits, which is precisely the regime our experiments probe. It is also the most intuitive test to run for a model billed as a foundation reward model. This gap is not unique to Robometer. Robo-Dopamine \citep{robodopamine} fine-tunes its reward model on the target task before RL, and RoboReward \citep{roboreward} reports no RL results on simulated benchmarks at all. The remaining evidence comes from real-robot experiments that are hard to reproduce, leaving the robustness of the reported gains an open question.

We introduce \textbf{RoboRef}, a vision-language reward model that builds on Robometer through three contributions. The first, and most central, concerns the training data. Robometer trains its two task heads, success and progress, only on \emph{successful} trajectories; failures reach the model solely through the preference head, as the lower-ranked side of a comparison. A model that is never trained to recognize failure has little reason to score it correctly, yet recognizing failure is exactly what an RL policy will stress-test. RoboRef therefore trains both the success and the progress head on success data \textit{and} failure data, and it does so by re-curating RBM-1M, which is the dataset Robometer was trained on. To our knowledge this updated dataset is the first training corpus at scale whose failures carry dense progress labels. Moreover, we set the preference head aside. The authors report that it helps even when both trajectories in a comparison succeed, but we argue its main value was to compensate for the two task heads never training on failures. Our data removes that limitation, so we drop the head for simplicity.  

Building that dataset is the hard part. Supervising the progress head on failures requires \emph{dense, per-frame} progress labels on failed attempts, and producing those labels is not trivial. For a successful trajectory, progress follows from how far along the demonstration has run. Robometer, like earlier work, labels frame $t$ of a length-$T$ demonstration with progress $t/T$, so the target climbs steadily from zero at the start to one at the end in a purely monotonic manner. A failed attempt has no such monotonic reference, since it may stall, regress, or briefly approach success before collapsing. We obtain labels from two complementary sources.

In simulation, we can read the simulator's ground-truth state at every step. This allows us to create artificial failure cases. We run scripted oracle policies and inject noise at chosen timesteps to produce varied failures, such as an arm that never reaches the object or a grasp that slips moments before the task is done. The simulator state tells us when to inject. To make the robot drop an object in a pick-and-place task, for instance, we perturb the gripper after the grasp is secured and before the task is complete. 

For real-world failures, where no such ground truth exists, we use a two-stage pipeline. First we split the video into 8 individual frames by sampling them uniformly. A VLM then reasons about what it sees in each frame, focusing on the main objects that are relevant to the task, as it is also given the task description and a fitting prompt. Then, a language model reads those descriptions and assigns a progress score, following a one-to-five rubric adapted from RoboReward \citep{roboreward}, where one means no progress and five means success. Separating the description step from the scoring step proved substantially more reliable than asking a single model to watch the whole video and output a number, which led to frequent hallucinations. The result is a sequence of frames, each tagged with a progress score from one to four, since the top level of the rubric marks completion and no failed attempt reaches it.

Our second contribution introduces in-context demonstrations to the reward model. In-context learning is the ability of a large pretrained model to take examples placed in its input and use them to perform a task at inference, without any change to its weights. It is among the defining capabilities of such models \citep{brown_language_2020}, and it improves few-shot generalization across both language \citep{dong_survey_2024} and modern multimodal reasoning tasks \citep{wang_qwen2-vl_2024}. For a reward model, a demonstration offers precisely what a textual task description cannot: a concrete and visual definition of what success looks like for a specific embodiment, scene, and camera viewpoint. We therefore give RoboRef the option to take a successful demonstration of the same task alongside the trajectory it evaluates. While prior methods have explored reading zero-shot values out of frozen models via in-context temporal ordering \citep{ma_vision_2025}, or by modifying their prompts \citep{gumbsch_demonstrations_2026}, building foundation reward models for robotic manipulation requires a more integrated approach. To our knowledge, we are the first to actively incorporate in-context demonstrations into the actual training loop of a robot foundation vision-language reward model. We append such a demonstration to each query during training, so that when one is provided the model grounds its judgment in a visual example instead of a potentially ambiguous instruction, which sharpens its sense of what task completion looks like.

Our third contribution revisits the training objective. In reinforcement learning the two ways of being wrong are not symmetric, and the more harmful one is the false positive \citep{gumbsch_demonstrations_2026}. When the reward declares success on a trajectory that has actually failed, it creates a spurious goal that an optimizing policy will actively seek out and exploit, the failure mode known as reward hacking. A false negative, withholding reward from genuine success or progress, is comparatively harmless. It can slow learning, since episodes run longer before a success signal ends them, but it does not hand the policy a shortcut to exploit. A reward model used for RL should therefore err toward caution to avoid being misled by false positives. We introduce a targeted modification of the standard objective that penalizes over-prediction more heavily than under-prediction, biasing the model toward conservative, harder-to-exploit predictions.

We evaluate RoboRef both offline and as the reward signal for downstream RL, across three simulated manipulation benchmarks. LIBERO \citep{liu_libero_2023} lets us compare directly with Robometer, which uses it for its own downstream evaluation. MetaWorld \citep{yu_meta-world_2019, mclean_meta-world_2025} is in-distribution for our reward model, and Robomimic \citep{robomimic} is out of distribution for both reward models. Our experiments revealed a sharp disconnect. Reward models that score well on standard offline metrics, such as the area under the curve (AUC) metric, can still fail completely as RL rewards, because an optimizing policy learns to exploit their false positives. RoboRef, by contrast, trains policies on tasks where the off-the-shelf baseline never learns at all, and delivers the strongest overall downstream performance of any reward model we test.

This thesis makes three main contributions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A failure-annotated reward dataset.} We re-curate and expand RBM-1M, the dataset behind Robometer, by labeling its failure trajectories with per-frame progress and adding failures from new sources. We generate simulated failures of physical understanding from scratch, and we annotate real-world failures with a dual VLM-then-LLM labeling pipeline. To our knowledge this is the first robot training corpus at scale with dense, per-frame progress labels on \emph{failure} trajectories.
    \item \textbf{In-context demonstrations for reward modeling.} We add in-context learning to the reward model as an optional mode, so it can be run either with a successful demonstration placed alongside the trajectory it scores or without one. This is a novel addition relative to prior vision-language reward models, and it grounds the model's notion of task success in a concrete example instead of a text instruction.
    \item \textbf{An asymmetric reward objective.} A targeted modification of the standard loss that addresses the documented reward hacking of overconfident vision-language reward models by penalizing false positives, yielding more conservative and less exploitable rewards.
\end{itemize}
We also report a finding that reaches beyond our own model. The offline metrics used by current vision-language reward models do not reliably predict their usefulness as downstream RL rewards, a gap we examine directly.

We organize these contributions around a set of research questions. Our main research question is the following.

\noindent\textit{To what extent can a fine-tuned vision-language model serve as a usable and generalizable reward signal for downstream robot reinforcement learning?}

\noindent We approach it through four sub-questions. The first three test one contribution each, the failure data, the demonstrations, and the loss, so that each design choice is judged on its own evidence. The fourth asks whether what they buy survives an optimizing policy, which is where the main question is decided.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does supervising the reward model on curated failure trajectories with dense per-frame progress labels improve its ability to discriminate success from failure?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} Do in-context demonstrations improve the reward model's judgment of task success?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Are vision-language reward models overconfident, and does an asymmetric loss that penalizes false positives make them more conservative and harder to exploit?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} Do these offline reward-quality gains transfer to downstream reinforcement learning, and if not, what is the failure mechanism?
\end{itemize}
